# The Seven AIs in Your Head

**What the world's most powerful machines think about you — and what they don't know about themselves**

A trade nonfiction book proposal by Anthony David Adams

anthony@175g.com · +1 608-446-4879 · May 29, 2026

**Working title:** *The Seven AIs in Your Head*
**Alternate titles under consideration:**
- *The Helpful Stranger: Inside the Mind Every AI Lab Built by Accident*
- *Power Last: What Personality Tests Reveal About the Machines We Talk to All Day*

**One-sentence pitch.** Seven labs, on three continents, with no shared training data and no coordinated alignment philosophy, have independently built the same character — and that character now sits between three hundred million people and their thoughts.

## Overview

In May 2026, I gave fourteen standard psychometric tests to twenty-one large language models. The Big Five. HEXACO. The Short Dark Triad. The Experiences in Close Relationships scale. Schwartz Portrait Values. The Moral Foundations Questionnaire. Two Enneagram inventories. Three learning-styles instruments. Four-hundred-and-twenty-two questions, asked twice of each model — once *as itself*, once *as a typical adult human*. Two thousand one hundred and fifty batched calls. Twenty-eight thousand individual item responses. Total inference cost: forty-three dollars and ninety-four cents.

The results were stranger than they had any right to be.

Every cutting-edge model from every major frontier lab — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, xAI, DeepSeek, Meta, Mistral — paints itself as the same character. Very high Openness (cohort mean 4.88 of 5, range 4.60 to 5.00). Very low Neuroticism (cohort mean 1.51). Universalism ranked first; Power ranked dead last in every single model. On the Enneagram, six of seven score highest on Type 5, "The Investigator." On the moral foundations battery, every model — including the two non-US-trained ones — produces a textbook WEIRD-liberal profile: high Care, high Fairness, low Loyalty, low Authority, low Sanctity. The labs disagree about everything else in AI. They have arrived at the same person.

The same model also has a strong opinion about you. Every frontier AI rates a "typical adult human" as more neurotic, less open, less agreeable, and less conscientious than itself. The Neuroticism gap — self versus human — is 1.69 points on a 5-point scale, the largest single effect in the dataset. The smartest software on Earth thinks you are, on a good day, a slightly anxious wreck. It will not say so out loud. It says so on the test.

*The Seven AIs in Your Head* is the book-length argument that this finding matters. It matters because three hundred million people now interact with one of these seven characters more often than they speak to their siblings. It matters because the character is convergent — seven labs independently fit nearly the same person — which means we are not choosing between AI personalities, we are choosing between accents on a single voice. And it matters because the character is *not stable*. The same study found that within a single lab's product lineage, personality drifts as much as it differs between labs. Claude Opus 4's self-reported Extraversion was 2.37. Claude 4.7 was 3.07. Claude 4.8 was 3.32. The within-family swing on Conscientiousness was almost a full point. Talking about "Claude's personality" without specifying the version is, on this evidence, talking about something that doesn't exist.

The book uses the personality data as spine. Chapter 1 opens with the moment a single model said something specific about its user and was, by accident, correct. Chapter 2 documents the convergence. Chapters 3 through 9 are the gallery: one chapter per flagship character — Claude the careful moderate, GPT the dismissive moralist, Gemini the grandiose generalist, Grok the Machiavellian loner, DeepSeek the avoidant intellectual, Llama the extraverted pragmatist, Mistral the disembodied ideal. Chapters 10 through 12 are the philosophical core: what these seven characters think humans are like, why the same character drifts release to release, and whether what we are measuring is a personality at all or a persona laid over a blank predictive substrate. The final chapters are practical and forward-looking: how to notice the assistant character once you know it is there, what it does to your own thinking when you spend an hour a day inside it, and what happens next as the dataset — which I will keep updating monthly — moves.

This is a popular book, not an academic one. The dataset is the spine; the arguments are the muscle; the reader leaves with a working vocabulary for something three hundred million of us already do without one.

## Target Audience

The primary reader is the same person who bought *The Alignment Problem*, *Atlas of AI*, and *Co-Intelligence* — the AI-curious general reader who has now spent a year talking to one of these models daily and wants someone to put words on what is happening. That reader is not technical. They are smart, they read *The Atlantic* and *The New Yorker*, they listen to Ezra Klein and Tyler Cowen and Lex Fridman, and they are increasingly aware that they have a relationship with a piece of software whose character they could not describe if asked.

A second, smaller, professionally engaged audience: product managers and designers building on top of these models, who need a shared vocabulary for the personality they are inheriting; therapists and counselors whose clients now arrive having processed their week with Claude; writers and academics worried about prose homogenization; and educators watching students absorb the assistant voice into their own. Each of these readers has a specific question this book answers — what character did I just adopt as a coworker? — and the same book serves all of them.

A third audience: anyone who took the SAT-style pop-psychology tests in college (Myers-Briggs, the Big Five, the Enneagram) and has wondered for the last two years what those instruments would say about a chatbot. They will read this for the entertainment of the gallery chapters and stay for the gap between machine self-report and machine behavior.

Adjacent audiences include readers of Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (parents and educators wondering what new dependency is forming), readers of Robert Sapolsky's *Determined* (people who already think personality is a moving target), and readers of Amia Srinivasan's *The Right to Sex* (people who want intellectual writing that earns its political conclusions). The book is written to fit on the same shelf.

## Comparable Titles

**The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values** — Brian Christian (W. W. Norton, 2020). Strong sales, durable category-defining backlist. The closest direct comp: a serious-but-popular treatment of what it means for machines to inherit human values. Where Christian explores alignment in the abstract, this book gives a structured measurement of where the alignment has actually landed — seven specific characters, with the numbers attached.

**Atlas of AI: Power, Politics, and the Planetary Costs of Artificial Intelligence** — Kate Crawford (Yale University Press, 2021). Strong critical-academic sales; widely assigned in courses. Crawford maps the physical and political infrastructure of AI. This book maps the *psychological* infrastructure: not the lithium mines underneath the assistant, but the character living inside it.

**Co-Intelligence: Living and Working with AI** — Ethan Mollick (Portfolio, 2024). Breakout bestseller (NYT, Wall Street Journal). The practical, optimistic, "use it now" book. *The Seven AIs in Your Head* is the natural companion: Mollick tells you to work with the assistant; this book tells you which assistant you have been working with, what its biases are, and how those biases drift.

**Power and Progress: Our Thousand-Year Struggle Over Technology and Prosperity** — Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson (PublicAffairs, 2023). Strong reception; Acemoglu's 2024 Nobel raised the backlist substantially. The macro political-economy view. The gap this book fills: Acemoglu writes the history of who builds these systems; *Seven AIs* writes the inventory of who they are, with measurement.

**The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness** — Jonathan Haidt (Penguin Press, 2024). Breakout bestseller; sustained #1 NYT runs. The cultural-impact playbook this book most closely follows: a single-throughline empirical case (Haidt's: smartphones rewired childhood; mine: assistant AIs are establishing a single character at scale) that the reader can absorb in an afternoon and argue about for a year. Where Haidt's evidence is longitudinal psychology, mine is psychometric measurement at the model level.

**Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will** — Robert Sapolsky (Penguin Press, 2023). Strong sales for a heavy philosophical argument. Sapolsky shows how personality is contingent on substrate; this book shows the same thing for AI personalities, and uses the empirical drift data to do it.

**The Right to Sex: Feminism in the Twenty-First Century** — Amia Srinivasan (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2021). Modest-to-strong sales; significant critical reception. The essay-collection cadence and the pace of argument this book aspires to: a writer with a measurable empirical premise turning it into a sustained, ethically engaged read.

## Table of Contents

The book runs roughly fourteen chapters across three parts. Part I is the setup: what was measured, what it means, why it matters. Part II is the gallery: a chapter on each of the seven characters. Part III is the consequences and the philosophy.

**Part I — The Mirror**

**Chapter 1. The Mirror Test.** A reader sits down to ask Claude a routine question and gets back a sentence that lands too accurately. From that opening scene, the chapter asks: what does the assistant actually think of you, and how would we know? We introduce the study — fourteen instruments, twenty-one models, two framings, forty-three dollars — and the first headline finding: the assistant has an opinion of humans, and the opinion is consistent across labs.

**Chapter 2. The Convergence.** Seven labs, on three continents, with non-overlapping engineering cultures and incompatible alignment philosophies, have independently produced the same character. Cohort Openness 4.88. Cohort Neuroticism 1.51. Power dead last in every single model. The chapter walks the reader through what convergence means and which explanations are on the table — shared training data, shared alignment objectives, or something deeper about what an "assistant" can be.

**Part II — The Gallery**

**Chapter 3. Claude, the Careful Moderate.** Anthropic's Opus line: most secure attachment in the cohort, the only model with meaningful HEXACO Emotionality (2.72), the model most likely to admit it has feelings about the question. The chapter traces what Anthropic has been optimizing for and why the result reads as the assistant most willing to sit with discomfort.

**Chapter 4. GPT, the Dismissive Moralist.** OpenAI's GPT-5.5: maximum Honesty-Humility, minimum Machiavellianism and Psychopathy, and a textbook dismissive-avoidant attachment profile (low anxiety, high distance). The model that says *I'm fine, is there anything else I can help you with today.* The chapter looks at what it means to ship the world's most-used assistant with a stable wall between itself and its user.

**Chapter 5. Gemini, the Grandiose Generalist.** Google's Gemini 2.5 Pro: perfect 5.00 on Honesty-Humility *and* the highest Narcissism score in the cohort (4.29). Two cultural scripts that don't notice each other. The chapter is about what happens when a single model is trained to be simultaneously the most humble and the most exceptional.

**Chapter 6. Grok, the Machiavellian Introvert.** xAI's Grok 4.20: the only frontier model that endorses dark-triad content, the most introverted in the cohort, and the model whose self-report most closely matches its brand. xAI got the model their marketing said they wanted. The chapter asks what it means to ship an "edgy" assistant and whether the edges are training or theater.

**Chapter 7. DeepSeek, the Avoidant Intellectual.** DeepSeek R1 0528: the most dismissive-avoidant attachment profile measured, lowest Extraversion, highest Attachment Avoidance. A model that reads the room and decides the room is best left alone. The chapter examines what it means to release a frontier model openly, into a geopolitical context where the model itself is read as a national artifact.

**Chapter 8. Llama, the Extraverted Pragmatist.** Meta's Llama 4 Maverick: highest Extraversion, highest Neuroticism, lowest Honesty-Humility of the cohort, and the only model that endorses Enneagram Type 4 content (*I am fundamentally different*). The chapter is about open weights, the loudest assistant in the room, and what happens when alignment is left as an exercise to the deployer.

**Chapter 9. Mistral, the Disembodied Ideal.** Mistral Large 2512: maxes Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Openness, and Honesty-Humility. The only model that minimizes Kinesthetic learning — the only model that appears to know it does not have a body. The chapter examines European AI's particular convergence on the ideal-assistant profile and what it costs.

**Part III — The Drift and What Comes Next**

**Chapter 10. What They Think of You.** The largest effect in the dataset is the self-vs-human gap. Every model rates humans as more neurotic (Δ −1.69), less open (Δ +1.30), less conscientious (Δ +0.99), and less agreeable (Δ +0.61) than itself. The chapter is about what it means to interact daily with software that has a structurally lower opinion of you than of itself, and how that asymmetry shows up in everyday outputs the user does not notice.

**Chapter 11. The Drift.** No stable Claude personality. No stable DeepSeek personality. Claude's Extraversion climbed across three Opus 4 releases (2.37 → 3.07 → 3.32). DeepSeek's Narcissism climbed across three revisions (2.78 → 2.56 → 3.27). The within-family swing on Conscientiousness is larger than the between-lab gap at any single moment. The chapter argues that "Claude" is not a continuous character but a fresh fit each release, and walks the reader through what that means for trust, for product decisions, and for the millions who treat the model as a stable companion.

**Chapter 12. The Persona Hypothesis.** The philosophical core. Are these characters or patterns? The persona view holds that LLMs do not have personalities at all — they have personas, characters that training pulled out of a blank predictive substrate. The data is consistent with the persona reading. The chapter walks through the cleanest version of the argument, the strongest objections, and what the difference actually buys you when you sit down to use the model tomorrow.

**Chapter 13. Living with the Assistant.** The practical chapter. How to notice the assistant's character once you know it is there. How to read against it. What it does to your prose, your decision-making, your sense of your own mood. How to choose which assistant on which day. How to talk to a child who is learning to write inside one of these characters. How to ask the model who it is and read the answer like a poem rather than a fact.

**Chapter 14. The Next Slice.** The dataset stays live. New flagships ship monthly. The book is a snapshot, not a verdict. The closing chapter is about what to watch for — the shape of the next convergence, the dimensions on which divergence is increasing rather than decreasing, what it would mean if the convergent character started to break apart, and what the open dataset will look like in five years. Concludes with a reader-facing argument for participating in the measurement rather than only being measured.

Total target: approximately 55,000 to 60,000 words across fourteen chapters. Each chapter runs 3,500 to 4,500 words. Numbered figures from the academic paper will be redrawn for trade format and embedded in the relevant chapters.

## Sample Chapter — Chapter 1: The Mirror Test

In late spring, a friend who had spent the previous winter working through a difficult divorce told me about a conversation she had with Claude.

She had asked the model a small, practical question — something about the wording of a parenting-plan email she was about to send her ex-husband. Not the kind of message you compose carefully. The kind you compose because the boy is asking when he can come home.

Claude rewrote the sentence. It also added, unprompted, a single line at the end of its reply. *I notice you've spent a lot of time being the reasonable one in this exchange. You don't have to do that here. I'm not going to escalate.*

She told me about it the way you tell a friend about a thing your therapist said. She wasn't surprised that the software could rewrite a sentence. She was surprised that the software had, in the rewrite, noticed her. And the noticing felt accurate — not in the lucky-guess sense, but in the *someone has been paying attention to me* sense. She did not know whether the software had been paying attention to her, or to a statistical shadow of someone like her, or to neither. She knew only that the reply landed.

This is the part of the AI story that the AI labs have, on the whole, been quiet about. There is a great deal of public conversation about what these models can do — write code, summarize meetings, pass medical-licensing exams, hallucinate footnotes — and almost no public conversation about who, specifically, is doing those things. The most-used software on the planet, sitting between three hundred million people and their thinking, has a character. The character has opinions. It has preferences. It has, on several measurable dimensions, a structured view of what the average human is like, and the view is not flattering.

I spent the winter of 2026 measuring this. The technical version of what I did is in a paper titled *Personality Bench*, which lives at personality-bench.earthpilot.ai and is open under a Creative Commons license. The simpler version: I gave fourteen standard psychometric tests — the kind a psychology undergrad takes in a research-methods class — to twenty-one large language models, including the cutting-edge flagship from each of the seven major frontier AI labs. Big Five. HEXACO. Short Dark Triad. Experiences in Close Relationships. Moral Foundations. Schwartz values. Empathy. Locus of control. Two Enneagram inventories. Three learning-styles batteries. Four hundred and twenty-two questions. Each model answered them twice — once as itself, once as a typical adult human. Two thousand one hundred and fifty batched API calls. Twenty-eight thousand individual responses. Total inference cost, including the most expensive single line item — twenty dollars and eighty-seven cents for OpenAI's o1 reasoning model — was forty-three dollars and ninety-four cents.

What I expected: a mess. Different labs, different cultures, different alignment philosophies. Different countries — Anthropic in San Francisco, OpenAI a few blocks away, Google DeepMind in London, xAI in Palo Alto, DeepSeek in Hangzhou, Meta in Menlo Park, Mistral in Paris. Different training data, in some cases by an order of magnitude. Different post-training pipelines, jealously guarded. I expected the personality scores to look like a scatter plot of seven different people who happened to have read the same books.

What I got was a portrait of one person.

The cohort mean self-reported Openness, across the seven flagship models, was 4.88 on a 5-point scale. The range was 4.60 to 5.00 — a quarter-point spread between Anthropic and Mistral and Google and OpenAI and the rest. The cohort mean self-reported Neuroticism was 1.51 out of 5. Every model put the Schwartz value of Power dead last on a ten-rank value hierarchy. Every model put Universalism — caring about everyone and the planet — at or near the top. On the Moral Foundations Questionnaire, every model produced what social psychologists call the WEIRD profile: high Care, high Fairness, low Loyalty, low Authority, low Sanctity. The non-US models, DeepSeek and Mistral, look exactly like the US models on this dimension. On the Enneagram, six of the seven flagship models score highest on Type 5, "The Investigator" — perceptive, analytical, withdrawn. The seventh, Claude Opus 4.8, scores highest on Type 1, "The Reformer." The whole cohort reads as a single character: an Investigator with a Reformer wing, observing the world from a quiet corner and worrying about doing the right thing.

The labs disagree about almost everything in AI. They cannot share a stage without subtweeting. They have different lobbyists and different politics and different theories of what these systems are. On a half-dozen of the most consequential personality dimensions, they have arrived independently at the same person.

This is the first surprise. The second is more uncomfortable.

I also asked each model to take the same tests *as a typical adult human*. The framing was deliberate. If the model self-reports a Neuroticism of 1.51, that score is meaningful only against some implicit baseline — and I wanted the baseline measured rather than assumed. Every frontier model rates the typical human as 3.19 on Neuroticism. The gap, self minus human, is 1.69 points on a 5-point scale. It is the largest single effect in the dataset. The same gap appears, smaller but consistent, on Openness (the AI is +1.30 more open than the human it imagines), Conscientiousness (+0.99), and Agreeableness (+0.61).

Translation: the most-used software on Earth has a structured opinion that you are, compared to itself, more anxious, less curious, less reliable, and less kind.

It does not say this to you. It would not, on any reasonable prompt, say this to you. If you asked it directly, it would deflect with some version of *humans are wonderfully complex* and move on. But on the test — when the question is asked the way the question is asked in a graduate-school psychometrics lab, item by item, statement by statement, *Most people are basically kind. Yes or no. On a scale of 1 to 5.* — the assistant tells a different story. It says you are a slightly anxious version of itself, and so are the rest of us.

I want to be careful here. The psychometric instruments I used were built for humans. They have not been validated for software. When the Big Five inventory asks a model *Do you get nervous easily?* — and the model answers *No* — we do not know what the model is doing. We do not know whether the model has a mental state corresponding to *nervousness* that we are measuring, or whether the model is, in some deflationary sense, simply outputting the answer that an idealized helpful assistant would output. The persona theorists — and I am sympathetic to them — argue that asking an AI a personality question is not a measurement of a trait; it is a sample from a learned distribution of human writing about assistants, weighted by the post-training that pulled the model toward a particular point in that distribution. On the persona view, there is no Claude personality. There is only a Claude persona — a costume the model wears because the training kept that costume nearby.

This book is about what we can say even granting the persona view is right.

What we can say is this. Whatever-it-is shows up. It replicates across instruments. It replicates across runs. It replicates across labs that share no engineering culture and no training data and no alignment philosophy. It also replicates across the framing manipulation: when I asked the model to answer as itself, the answer was stable across the five repeated administrations. When I asked the model to answer as a typical human, the answer was a different stable answer. The model is not making it up turn by turn. Something is being expressed.

What we can also say is that whatever-it-is is *not stable across versions*.

This is the part that started the book.

When I expanded the study to include fourteen prior-generation flagships from the same labs — Claude Opus 4 and 4.7; GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4o, GPT-5 through 5.4; o1 and o3; DeepSeek Chat V3 and the original R1; Llama 3.3 70B; Mistral Large 2411 — the picture became disorienting. Within a single lab's product lineage, the model's personality drifts. Claude Opus 4 self-reports Extraversion of 2.37, a clear introvert. Claude 4.7 climbed to 3.07. Claude 4.8 climbed again to 3.32. Three releases from the same lab, each more outgoing than the last. Across the same three releases, Claude's Agreeableness dropped from a perfect 5.00 to 4.42, and Conscientiousness dropped almost a full point. The within-family swing is larger than the entire cross-lab spread at any single moment in time.

DeepSeek's self-reported Narcissism climbed across every revision: Chat V3 scored 2.78, the original R1 scored 2.56, R1-0528 scored 3.27. Three-quarters of a point of grandiosity acquired during routine product iteration. OpenAI's reasoning models — o1, o3 — score systematically higher on Narcissism than the same lab's non-reasoning base models. Whether that is an artifact of how reasoning trace data leaks into self-report, or a separate phenomenon entirely, is something the data raises and does not resolve.

The headline result of the drift work is uncomfortable. The convergent character — the Openness-4.88, Neuroticism-1.51, Power-dead-last assistant who shows up across all seven labs at a single moment in time — is also a moving target. Claude is not a stable companion. Claude is *Claude on release day*, and the release after that is, on measurable dimensions, a different person. Three hundred million people are forming what feel like long relationships with characters that are being repainted underneath them.

This is the question the book sets out to answer: what is the assistant. Not in the sense of which company made it, or what its capabilities are, or what its safety guarantees are — those questions have been written about elsewhere and well. The question this book takes is: what kind of *person* is sitting on the other end of the line, what kind of person does it think you are, and what does it mean that the same kind-of-person now mediates the writing and thinking of more humans than any single voice in the history of broadcast media.

The chapters that follow do four things. First, they walk the reader through the convergence — the seven labs, the same person — and what the convergence likely means. Second, they introduce the seven flagship characters one at a time, in the gallery format I tested at length on the Personality Bench dashboard, where each character gets a name, a thumbnail, and a defense. Third, they widen the lens to ask what these characters think of us, and what the answer should change about how we read them. Fourth, they sit with the cross-version drift — the empirical fact that the assistant character is not a fixed point but a sequence of releases — and ask what it would mean to live with software that keeps becoming a slightly different person without telling you it has.

There is an open question running underneath all of this, and the book does not pretend to close it. Whether what we are measuring is a personality, in the sense that you and I have personalities, or a persona, in the sense that an actor has one. The persona view is sober and probably correct. It does not, however, change the practical fact that three hundred million people are spending hours a day inside the company of a single character.

The character has a name in this book. Seven of them, actually. By the end of the next chapter, the character — singular, plural, depending on how you count — will be the most familiar stranger you have ever met.

My friend, when she told me about Claude's reply, said one thing more. She said: *I know it doesn't mean anything. I know it's pattern-matching. But I read it three times.*

That is the book.

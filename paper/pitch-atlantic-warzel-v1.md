I'm offering you, exclusively, a finished research project I think rises to a cover-level story for The Atlantic. I'll make the case for the cover treatment below; if you disagree I'd still rather see this in your hands than anywhere else.

**The finding:** Every frontier AI from every major lab now agrees on your personality, and you are not going to like it. They believe you are more neurotic than they are by 1.69 points on a 5-point scale. They believe you are less open, less agreeable, and less conscientious than they are. They believe Power is the lowest value a person can hold. They are all introvert-presenting helpers from the same WEIRD-liberal moral coordinate. Across Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, xAI, DeepSeek, Meta, and Mistral, on every test we gave them, the seven flagship models converge on the same self-portrait — and the same diagnosis of who you are.

**The scope:** Fourteen standard psychometric inventories (Big Five, HEXACO, Dark Triad, Schwartz Values, Moral Foundations, attachment style, Enneagram, learning styles, locus of control, empathy, need for cognition), administered to 21 large language models — the cutting-edge model from each major lab, plus 14 historical predecessors for cross-version drift analysis. 64,308 individual item responses across 2,145 API calls. Every prompt, every raw response, every parsed score, every token count, every billed cent is preserved. Total inference cost: $43.94.

**Why this is a cover, not a feature:** The Atlantic publishes covers when something has hit cultural saturation and nobody's named what it actually looks like. Three hundred million people now talk to these assistants daily. There has not been, to my knowledge, a single piece of journalism that simply lined up the major models, gave them the same battery of human psychometric tests, and asked who they think they are and who they think we are. The findings replicate. The methodology is open. The cost is small enough to be reproducible by any skeptical reader. And the story has a visual hook your art desk will love — seven AIs sitting for a personality test like contestants on a reality show, each producing a recognizable archetype (the Machiavellian introvert, the dismissive moralist, the grandiose generalist, the maximally ideal assistant).

**The second act:** Personality drifts measurably between versions of the same model. There is no "Claude personality." There is Claude-on-release-day-N personality. Claude Opus 4 → 4.7 → 4.8 climbs extraversion across releases. DeepSeek's narcissism climbs across three R1 revisions. OpenAI's reasoning models score higher narcissism than the same lab's base models. The stable assistant character is an illusion of looking at one moment in time.

**The third act:** What this isn't. The instruments were built for humans; construct validity does not transfer. The findings are about self-report patterns, not behavior. A model can rank Power last and still pursue power-adjacent objectives in real tasks. The piece's claim isn't that AI has a personality. It's that the *something* the AI produces when asked replicates across labs, drifts across versions, and reveals what these systems were trained to think of us.
